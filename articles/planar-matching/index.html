A question in motion

How many times can the best match change?

Astrée Iriart5 min read · 2:20 film

The question

Pair each point of one planar set with a point of another, so that the total squared distance is as small as possible. Now let one set turn about its centre. As it turns, the best pairing changes. How often?

A small answer to a large search

Günter Rote presented the rotational counting question in 2008, tracing it back to Karel Zikan in 1991. There are n! possible pairings: for ten points, more than three and a half million. The sharp bound proved in the accompanying manuscript is much smaller. For n ≥ 2, at most n(n − 1) different pairings can be uniquely optimal during one full turn. For ten points, that is ninety.

The word “uniquely” matters. Counting all pairings involved in a tie can give n! even without any motion. The paper also gives a specified tie-breaking rule with a bounded number of selected pairings; arbitrary tie-breaking does not have that guarantee.

02 /

A change of picture

The argument starts by replacing each pairing with a single point in a new plane. Its two coordinates record how well the pairing aligns the sets along two perpendicular rotation phases. As one set turns, choosing the best pairing becomes choosing the farthest point of this cloud in a sweeping direction.

Only corners of the cloud’s convex hull can be strict winners. A corner may represent several tied pairings, and an entire edge may be optimal at a transition. Even with such degeneracies, the polygon has at most n(n − 1) distinct vertices. The factorial cloud has a quadratic outline.

03 /

The simplest arrangement is already extreme

Place the fixed points at distinct positions on a line. The best pairing sorts the moving points by their shadows on that line. Each pair of distinct moving points exchanges projection order twice per turn.

Choose the moving points so that no two of these exchange times coincide. Then the n(n − 1) exchanges produce that many distinct strict projection orders, each with its own uniquely optimal pairing. This is an existence construction, not a claim that every collinear arrangement attains the bound. For n ≥ 3, small perturbations preserve all the strict winners, so both sets can be noncollinear and still reach the same limit.

04 /

Where the count comes from

During one full rotation, every pair of moving points winds once. There are n(n − 1)/2 pairs: together they contribute n(n − 1) half-turns. The proof shows why this total limits how often the optimum can switch.

First consider generic data, where switches are isolated. Relabel the moving points by their current partners, and connect the relabelled configurations across each switch using the proof’s small collision-free bridges. The resulting loop stays in a convex region where one fixed pairing is strictly optimal. Inside that region there is no collision to wind around, so the loop’s total winding is zero.

The bridges therefore cancel the physical winding. Each switch contributes a positive integer charge, measured in half-turns, of at least one. Their total is n(n − 1), so there cannot be more switches than that. Longer cycles can consume more than one unit. Perturbation then transfers the bound on distinct strict winners to nongeneric inputs.

The four-point illustration uses twelve half-turns and twelve switches of charge one. The general theorem goes further than rigid rotation: collision-free periodic motions with monotone pairwise angles are controlled by their total pair winding.

05 /

Why the plane matters

In three dimensions, a direction can move around on a sphere instead of winding around a circle. The planar counting argument no longer applies. That alone would not disprove the bound, but an explicit counterexample does: four points rotating about a fixed axis have at least thirteen distinct uniquely optimal pairings, exceeding the planar limit of twelve.

The paper certifies thirteen strict winners at exact rational rotations. It does not claim that thirteen is the exact total, or settle the growth rate of the three-dimensional problem.

06 /

From counting to alignment

Once the correlation polygon is known, exact alignment is simple to express: choose a vertex farthest from the origin and recover its best rotation and translation. For rational planar coordinates, the paper enumerates the polygon in polynomial time using at most 2V exact assignment queries, where V ≤ n(n − 1). It is not necessary to enumerate all n! pairings.

This gives a globally optimal orientation-preserving rigid alignment and a realizing pairing. A separate branch handles reflections. The contribution is the worst-case bound and its algorithmic guarantee; exact registration methods themselves predate this work.

Film transcript Original narration

Two sets of points lie in a plane. Pair them up, one to one, so that the total squared distance between partners is as small as possible. Now let one set turn about its centre. As it turns, the best pairing changes.

In 2008, the mathematician Günter Rote asked: during one full turn, how many different best pairings can there be? The number of pairings grows like n factorial. For ten points, that is more than three and a half million.

The key is a change of picture. Each pairing becomes a single point in the plane. Turning the set sweeps a direction around this cloud, and the best pairing is always the point farthest in that direction. So only corners of the outline can ever win. Here, twelve of the twenty-four.

The answer: the outline never has more than n times n minus one corners, wherever the points are placed. For ten points, ninety.

Nothing smaller would do. Put the fixed points on a line, and the best pairing simply sorts the moving points by their shadows. Each pair of shadows changes order twice per turn. Six pairs, twelve changes: the simplest arrangement is already the extreme one.

The count also has a reason. In one turn, every pair of moving points turns once around. Six pairs: twelve half-turns. Follow the points by their current partners. That arrangement always stays where its pairing is optimal, a region with no hole to wind around. So all the turning must be given back by switches, and each switch gives back at least half a turn. Twelve half-turns, twelve switches. The account balances.

The reason is planar, and so is the result. In space, directions can travel without winding, and four points turning about an axis give thirteen best pairings, one more than the plane allows.

One practical consequence: the best rigid alignment of two planar point sets with unknown correspondence can be computed exactly, by checking at most n times n minus one candidates.
